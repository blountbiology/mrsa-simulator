MRSA: When Antibiotics Stop Working

Staphylococcus aureus (staph) is a type of bacteria that can cause skin infections and sometimes serious illness. Antibiotics can treat many staph infections, but some staph bacteria resist certain antibiotics. These resistant bacteria are called MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus). MRSA infections can be harder to treat.

Why does MRSA matter?

MRSA has caused serious infections in hospitals and communities. Infection prevention and improvements in healthcare helped reduce many MRSA infections, but the problem has not disappeared.

MRSA: From a rare infection to a public health concern

Illustrative trend

MRSA became more common over several decades. Better infection control—including handwashing, screening, and isolation precautions—helped reduce serious infections. Improvements in medical care also contributed, but MRSA remains a concern today.

Your investigation

You are part of a hospital's infectious disease team. Use a model to investigate how antibiotic exposure changes a population of staph bacteria over generations.

You will complete five cycles. In each cycle, apply the antibiotic, observe which bacteria survive, and let the survivors reproduce. Record the population only at the start and after reproduction. Then use your numbers to make a graph and explain your findings on your handout.

This is a simplified model of natural selection, not a medical treatment plan.
